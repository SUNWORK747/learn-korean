What Are Particles? 조사란 무엇인가

The General Concept — A Word Family English Doesn't Have

1Concept Introduction — The Missing Piece Behind Flexible Word Order

In GRAM-PRE-013 you learned that Korean sentences follow SOV order (Subject-Object-Verb) instead of English's SVO order, and that Korean word order is actually more flexible than English's — words can often be reordered without changing the basic meaning of the sentence. But that raises an obvious question: if word position doesn't reliably tell you "who did what to whom," what does?

The answer is 조사 (particles) — a small family of grammatical markers that English simply does not have. A particle attaches directly to the end of a noun or pronoun, with no space, and its job is to label that word's role in the sentence: is it the topic, the subject, the object, the location, the destination? In this chapter you will learn the general idea of what a particle is and why Korean relies on them so heavily. You will not yet learn the detailed usage rules of any single particle — that begins with GRAM-BEG-001.

Preview
English: "I eat rice." — word order alone tells you "I" is the subject and "rice" is the object.
Korean: 저는 밥을 먹어요. (jeoneun bap-eul meogeoyo) — here 는 marks 저 ("I") as the topic/subject, and 을 marks 밥 ("rice") as the object. The particles do the job that word order does in English.

2Concept Explanation

2.1 What exactly is a 조사 (particle)?

A particle is a short, suffix-like grammatical word that attaches directly to the end of a noun or pronoun, with no space in writing. It does not carry meaning on its own the way a noun or verb does — instead, it marks the grammatical role that the noun in front of it is playing in the sentence.

Noun + particle = one attached unit (no space)
Noun+ Particle= ResultRole marked
저는저는topic/subject ("as for me")
밥을밥을object ("rice," the thing eaten)
학교에학교에location/direction ("to school")
친구가친구가subject ("friend," as the doer)

Notice that the particle is never written as a separate word with a space before it — 저는 is one written unit, not 저 는. This is why particles are often described as "attached" rather than "separate" words.

3Examples

3.1 Same meaning, reordered words — because the particles don't move

Because each particle stays attached to its noun, reordering the nouns in a Korean sentence does not change who is doing what to whom.

저I
는topic
밥rice
을object
먹어요eat
밥rice
을object
저I
는topic
먹어요eat

Both rows above mean the same thing, "I eat rice" — only the emphasis shifts slightly. Compare that to English: reordering "I eat rice" into "Rice eat I" destroys the meaning, because English has no particle to keep "rice" marked as the object once it moves to the front.
