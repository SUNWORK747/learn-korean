1Concept Introduction — Two Number Systems

This is the last chapter of Elementary Korean, and it covers something English speakers find genuinely surprising: Korean has two complete sets of numbers that exist side by side, and which one you use depends entirely on what you're counting. One set comes from Chinese characters and is called Sino-Korean (일, 이, 삼, 사…) — you already met it briefly in GRAM-PRE-021. The other is native to Korean and is called Native-Korean (하나, 둘, 셋, 넷…). Neither system is "more correct" — they're just used for different things, and a Korean counting noun (called a 단위명사, "counter/classifier") almost always demands one specific system.

The good news is that the rule is learnable: money, minutes, dates, and floors use Sino-Korean numbers; most everyday physical things you can count — items, people, age, o'clock, animals, cups — use Native-Korean numbers. And when a Native-Korean number sits directly in front of a counter, five of them (하나, 둘, 셋, 넷, 스물) shrink into a shorter form. That contraction is the last piece of the puzzle this chapter teaches.

Preview
사과 한 개 주세요. ("Please give me one apple.")
이거 삼천 원이에요. ("This is 3,000 won.")
한 개 ("one item") uses the Native-Korean number 하나, contracted to 한, with the counter 개. 삼천 원 ("3,000 won") uses the Sino-Korean number 삼천 with the counter 원 (money) — a completely different number system, chosen because the counter is different.

2Concept Explanation

2.1 The two number systems, side by side

Sino-Korean numbers build in a very regular, almost mathematical way (like place-value blocks); Native-Korean numbers are a separate vocabulary set that generally only goes up to 99 in everyday use (beyond that, Korean switches to Sino-Korean even for things that would normally take Native-Korean counters).

Study Tip Sino-Korean numbers reuse the same syllables you already know from dates and phone numbers (일, 이, 삼…). Native-Korean numbers are a completely separate word list you simply have to memorize — think of them like irregular vocabulary, not a formula.

사과 한 개 ("one apple") — noun + [contracted Native-Korean number] + [counter]. Korean number phrases always follow this word order: Noun → Number → Counter, not Number → Noun as in English.
