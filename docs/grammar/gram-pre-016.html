Forming Questions 의문문 만들기

No Word-Order Change — Just Ending, Intonation, and Punctuation

GRAM-PRE-016 Beginner (Pre-TOPIK) 20 min
LevelBeginner (for students who already know the -아/어요 and -ㅂ니다/습니다 endings from GRAM-PRE-015)
CategoryBasic Sentence Structure > 2. Basic Sentence Structure
Learning Goals ① State the core rule: Korean yes/no questions are formed WITHOUT any word-order inversion — simply add a question mark in writing and rising intonation in speech to the same sentence structure
② Recognize the -아/어요? pattern for polite casual questions, and the -ㅂ니까?/습니까? pattern for formal questions
③ Form wh-questions using Korean question words (누구, 뭐/무엇, 어디, 언제, 왜, 어떻게, 얼마) placed in the same position the answer word would occupy, unlike English's fronted "wh-movement"

1Concept Introduction — Same Words, Different Ending

In GRAM-PRE-015 you learned that Korean sentences end in four possible types — statement (평서문), question (의문문), command (명령문), and suggestion (청유문) — and that at the polite casual (-아/어요) speech level, the statement and question forms can look completely identical in writing, distinguished only by a question mark and, in speech, by rising intonation. This chapter picks up exactly where that left off and shows you, in detail, how Korean actually builds a question out of a statement.

If you are used to English, this is going to feel almost too easy: English turns "You go to school." into a question by flipping the subject and the helping verb ("Do you go to school?"), or by moving a question word to the very front ("Where do you go?"). Korean does neither. The words stay in the exact same order, and (depending on speech level) the ending either stays the same or changes only its final consonant — never its position in the sentence.

Preview
학교에 가요. (hakgyo-e gayo, "You go to school.")
학교에 가요? (hakgyo-e gayo?, "Do you go to school?")
Same string of words, same word order — only the punctuation (and, in speech, the rising pitch on the last syllable) tells you it is a question.

2Concept Explanation

2.1 Yes/No questions at the -아/어요 level — ending stays the same

At the polite casual speech level (해요체, -아/어요), Korean makes a yes/no question by changing nothing about the words themselves. The subject, object, and verb all stay exactly where they were in the statement. Only two things signal that the sentence is now a question:

SignalWhere it appearsStatementQuestion
PunctuationIn writing. (period)? (question mark)
IntonationIn speechFlat / falling pitch on the last syllableRising pitch on the last syllable
Statement
학교에 가요.
"You go to school."
→
Question
학교에 가요?
"Do you go to school?"
Key Rule No auxiliary verb like English "do/does" is inserted, and nothing moves to the front. The sentence 학교에 가요 is literally the same string of characters whether it ends in a period or a question mark — the grammar does not change, only the punctuation/tone does.
