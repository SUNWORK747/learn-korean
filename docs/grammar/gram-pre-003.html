1Concept Introduction — The Sounds That Start a Block

In GRAM-PRE-001 you learned that every Hangul syllable block begins with an initial consonant slot (초성), and in GRAM-PRE-002 you learned the vowels that fill the block's required center slot (중성). Now it's time to complete the picture: the consonants that can open (and sometimes close) a syllable block. Korean has 14 basic consonants, and — just like the vowels — they were not designed randomly. GRAM-PRE-001 introduced Hangul's core design principle, iconicity: each letter's shape is a picture of something real. For consonants, that "something real" is the shape of your mouth and tongue when you say the sound.

Beyond the 14 basic consonants, Korean also has 5 "tense" consonants (경음), written by doubling a plain letter. Together, the 14 basic consonants and 5 tense consonants make up all 19 consonant letters in the Korean alphabet. In this chapter you will learn to recognize, classify, and pronounce all of them, including the three-way plain/tense/aspirated contrast that changes the meaning of otherwise identical-looking words.

Preview
불 (bul, "fire"), 뿔 (ppul, "horn"), and 풀 (pul, "grass") look almost identical, but each uses a different member of the same consonant family — plain ㅂ, tense ㅃ, and aspirated ㅍ — and they are three completely different words. Learning to hear and produce this three-way contrast is one of the biggest early hurdles for English speakers, since English does not use it this way.

2Concept Explanation

2.1 The 14 basic consonants

Each basic consonant has its own romanization and can sound slightly different depending on whether it appears at the start of a syllable (initial, 초성) or at the end (final, 종성 / 받침) — this final-position behavior will be covered in detail in GRAM-PRE-004. For now, focus on the sound each letter makes as an initial consonant.

3Examples

3.1 The plain / tense / aspirated three-way contrast

These three real-word sets show the same base sound (b/p, d/t, and j/ch) produced three different ways. Notice that each set is a completely different word with a completely different meaning — the plain/tense/aspirated distinction is not a minor accent detail, it changes what you are saying.

Try It
Say 불 with relaxed lips and almost no puff of air, then 뿔 with your throat tight and clenched, then 풀 with a strong burst of air (imagine blowing out a candle). Feeling the difference in your throat and breath is often more reliable than trying to "hear" the difference at first.
