-(으)ㅁ Nominalizer & Formal Written Ending

From Noun-Forming Suffix to a Terse, Written-Style Sentence Ender

GRAM-ADV-001 Advanced (TOPIK II) 35 min
LevelAdvanced (고급, TOPIK II 5–6급) — 1st item of this stage, and the 1st item of the "문어체·격식 표현" (Formal/Written Expressions) section, opening Advanced grammar right after the Intermediate finale, GRAM-INT-034
CategoryFormal/Written Expressions > 1. 문어체·격식 표현
Learning Goals ① Understand that -(으)ㅁ is the nominalizer suffix, and recognize its two distinct jobs: a noun-forming suffix that creates fixed dictionary words (기쁨, 죽음, 믿음), and a productive, terse sentence-final ending used in formal written Korean
② Correctly form -(으)ㅁ: vowel-final and ㄹ-final stems take -ㅁ (ㄹ drops and becomes the batchim), while consonant-final stems take -음
③ Recognize the blunt, impersonal tone -(으)ㅁ conveys as a sentence ending, and identify the written contexts where it replaces -습니다/-어요 (news headlines, notices, diaries, academic abstracts, signage, official reports)

1Concept Introduction — Entering Advanced, Written-Register Korean

Welcome to Advanced (고급, TOPIK II 5–6급) Korean. Every grammar point up through GRAM-INT-034 -(으)ㄹ까 하다 belonged to the world of spoken, conversational Korean — the kind you'd use with friends, coworkers, or a teacher. Starting here, the grammar shifts into 문어체 (written/literary register): the language of essays, news articles, official documents, academic papers, and formal announcements. These patterns aren't wrong in speech, but native speakers reserve most of them for writing, and using them well is what separates an intermediate learner from an advanced one.

-(으)ㅁ is the perfect opening item for this shift, because you've technically already met it — it's the same nominalizer (명사형 어미) that turns a verb or adjective into a noun, the way -기 also does. You may already know fixed, lexicalized nouns like 기쁨 ("joy," from 기쁘다) or 죽음 ("death," from 죽다). What's new at the Advanced level is -(으)ㅁ's second job: standing alone at the very end of a sentence, replacing -습니다 or -어요 entirely, to produce a short, blunt, official-sounding statement. You'll see this constantly in news headlines, building notices, diary entries, and research abstracts.

Preview
관계자 외 출입을 금함. ("Entry prohibited except for authorized personnel." — a sign)
특이사항 없음. ("Nothing unusual to report." — an inspection log)
Neither sentence has a subject, a verb ending like -습니다, or a period-softening tone — just a stem plus -(으)ㅁ. That terseness is the whole point: it reads as a flat, formal record or notice rather than something a person is saying to you directly.

2Concept Explanation

2.1 Form: -ㅁ after vowels/ㄹ, -음 after consonants

-(으)ㅁ attaches directly to a verb or adjective stem, and which shape it takes depends only on how the stem ends. Stems ending in a vowel simply add -ㅁ as a new final consonant (batchim). Stems ending in ㄹ also just add -ㅁ — but the ㄹ itself disappears, because a syllable block can't hold two final consonants, so ㅁ takes its place. Stems ending in any other consonant add the full syllable -음.

-(으)ㅁ by stem type
Stem typeRuleExampleResult
Vowel-finalstem + -ㅁ가다 ("to go")감
ㄹ-finaldrop ㄹ, stem + -ㅁ살다 ("to live"), 만들다 ("to make")삶, 만듦
Consonant-finalstem + -음먹다 ("to eat"), 있다 ("to exist/have")먹음, 있음
이다/아니다이 + -ㅁ / 아니 + -ㅁ학생이다 ("to be a student")학생임
Study Tip Past tense simply inserts -았/었- before -음, the same way it does everywhere else: 왔음 ("came"), 완료되었음 ("has been completed"), 없었음 ("there was none"). Because -음 always starts with a consonant sound, the past-tense marker never contracts away — you'll see -았음/-었음/-였음 as a fixed chunk throughout formal writing.
