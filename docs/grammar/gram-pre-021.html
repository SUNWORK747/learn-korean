Sino-Korean Numbers vs. Native Korean Numbers

1Concept Introduction — Why Korean Has Two Sets of Numbers

Unlike English, which has only one set of numbers for everything, Korean uses two completely different number systems side by side, and choosing the right one depends on what you are counting. One system came into Korean from Chinese characters long ago and is called Sino-Korean numbers (한자어 수사); the other is native to the Korean language itself and is called Native Korean numbers (고유어 수사). Both sets are used constantly in everyday life — you cannot avoid either one.

The good news is that the split follows a fairly learnable pattern: Sino-Korean numbers are used for "structured" counting like dates, prices, and phone numbers, while Native Korean numbers are used for counting people, objects, and age with a counter word. This chapter teaches both full sets 1–10, the rule for choosing between them, and the small but important form changes that four Native Korean numbers (and one multiple of ten) undergo right before a counter.

Preview
사과 한 개 (sagwa han gae, "one apple") uses a Native Korean number (하나→한) because it counts an object with a counter word, while 전화번호 (jeonhwabeonho, "phone number") is always read digit by digit in Sino-Korean — the same word "one" is spoken completely differently depending on what it is counting.

2Concept Explanation

2.1 Sino-Korean numbers (한자어 수사), 1–10

Sino-Korean numbers derive from Chinese numerals. They are simple, regular, and — once you learn 1–10 — easy to extend to any larger number, because Korean counts in the same base-10 blocks that the numerals themselves are built from.

Sino-Korean numbers 1–10

4사sa

Sino-Korean numbers are used for: dates (year, month, day), money and prices, phone numbers, minutes when telling time, math and counting in sequence (1, 2, 3... like counting off), and floor numbers in a building.

3Examples

3.1 Telling time — both systems in one phrase

The clearest illustration that Korean mixes both systems naturally is telling time: the hour uses Native Korean, while the minutes use Sino-Korean — in the very same sentence.

네 시 삼십 분
=
네 시hour: 넷→네 (Native Korean)
+
삼십 분minutes: 삼십=30 (Sino-Korean)

네 시 삼십 분 means "4:30." Koreans never say the hour in Sino-Korean or the minutes in Native Korean — the hour unit (시) always pairs with Native Korean, and the minute unit (분) always pairs with Sino-Korean.
