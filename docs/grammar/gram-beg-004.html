의 Possessive Particle

"'s" / "of" — and Its Common Contracted Forms

GRAM-BEG-004 Elementary (TOPIK I) 15 min
LevelElementary (TOPIK I, 1–2급)
CategoryParticles > 1. Particles
Learning Goals ① Use 의 in the pattern Noun1 + 의 + Noun2 to mean "Noun1's Noun2"
② Recognize the extremely common contracted forms 저의→제 ("my," polite) and 나의→내 ("my," casual), and know these contractions are used far more often than the full 의 form in speech
③ Recognize that 의 is frequently OMITTED entirely in casual speech when the possessive relationship is already clear from context

1Concept Introduction — Showing Possession

English shows possession with "'s" ("my friend's bag") or "of" ("the name of the teacher"). Korean has one small particle that does this job: 의 (ui). Attach it between two nouns — Noun1 + 의 + Noun2 — and you get "Noun1's Noun2," in exactly that order, just like in English.

There's a twist, though: in actual spoken Korean, the full 의 form is often contracted (especially with "I/me") or even dropped entirely when the possessive meaning is already obvious. This chapter covers all three faces of 의 — the full pattern, its near-mandatory contractions, and when it simply disappears.

Preview
친구의 가방 (chinguui gabang, "[my] friend's bag") and 선생님의 책 (seonsaengnimui chaek, "the teacher's book") show the plain Noun1 + 의 + Noun2 pattern — but when the "owner" is "I," Koreans almost never say 저의/나의 out loud. They say 제 or 내 instead, as you'll see below.

2Concept Explanation

2.1 The basic pattern: Noun1 + 의 + Noun2

의 attaches right after the "owner" noun, with a space before the noun that follows it. The order matches English "'s": the possessor comes first, the thing possessed comes second.

Noun1의 Noun2 — "Noun1's Noun2"
Noun1 (owner)+ 의Noun2 (possessed)Meaning
친구 (friend)친구의가방 (bag)"[my] friend's bag"
선생님 (teacher)선생님의책 (book)"the teacher's book"
한국 (Korea)한국의수도 (capital)"Korea's capital"
동생 (younger sibling)동생의방 (room)"[my] younger sibling's room"
Study Tip Unlike most particles you've seen so far, 의 never changes form based on batchim — it's always just 의, attached with no space, and then a space before Noun2. The hard part of this chapter isn't the spelling rule — it's knowing when native speakers shrink or drop it, which is what sections 2.2 and 2.3 cover.
