When Something Happens To You, Not Because Of You

Learning Goals ① Understand what passive voice does in Korean — shifting the sentence's focus from the doer of an action to the person or thing affected by it — and recognize the two systems: irregular suffixes (이/히/리/기) and the productive -아/어지다

1Concept Introduction — Shifting the Focus of a Sentence

In English, you turn an active sentence into a passive one mostly by rearranging word order and adding "be + past participle": "The dog bit the man" becomes "The man was bitten by the dog." Korean has passive voice too (피동, pidong), but it builds it very differently. Instead of a helper verb, Korean either inserts one of four short suffixes — 이, 히, 리, 기 — directly into the verb stem, or attaches the ending -아/어지다 after the verb stem. Both turn an active, "doing" verb into a passive, "having something done to it / happening on its own" verb, moving the spotlight away from whoever performed the action and onto the person or thing affected by it.

Here's the part that makes this one of the harder topics in intermediate Korean: there is no single fixed rule for which of the four suffixes attaches to which verb. It's essentially a closed, historical list — you have to memorize each passive verb individually, the same way English learners memorize irregular past tenses like "go → went" rather than applying "add -ed." The -아/어지다 pattern, by contrast, is fully productive and predictable: you can attach it to almost any verb stem the same way every time, and it does double duty — forming a passive for verbs that don't have a suffix passive, and expressing "becoming" a certain state when attached to adjectives. This chapter covers both systems side by side, and how to tell them apart in real sentences.

Preview
문이 저절로 열렸어요. ("The door opened by itself.")
날씨가 많이 추워졌어요. ("The weather has gotten a lot colder.")
열렸어요 (열다 + suffix 리 → 열리다) is a suffix passive: the door didn't open itself on purpose, something happened to it. 추워졌어요 (춥다 + -아/어지다) uses the same -아/어지다 pattern, but here on an adjective, to describe a gradual change of state. Different jobs, one shared idea: the subject isn't in control — something is happening to it.

2Concept Explanation

2.1 What "passive" does to a sentence

Compare an active sentence with its passive counterpart: 경찰이 도둑을 잡았어요. ("The police caught the thief.") is active — 경찰 (police) is the subject doing the catching, and 도둑 (thief) is the object being caught. Turn it passive: 도둑이 경찰에게 잡혔어요. ("The thief was caught by the police.") Now 도둑 becomes the subject, the verb changes from 잡다 to its passive form 잡히다, and the original doer 경찰 is marked with -에게/한테 ("by") — or simply dropped if it's unknown or unimportant, which is extremely common in natural Korean.

How roles shift from active to passive
RoleIn the active sentenceIn the passive sentence
Doer of the actionSubject, marked -이/가Optional "by" phrase, marked -에게/한테 (people) or -에 (natural forces) — often dropped
Thing affectedObject, marked -을/를Becomes the new subject, marked -이/가
